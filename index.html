People’s Vertex

An interactive installation built for New Sun Rising, a Pittsburgh nonprofit. We designed an experiential future: a scenario where small nonprofits have been pushed underground, and People’s Vertex is the secret hub where they keep each other alive.

Problem
Nonprofits under pressure
Hard to feel from the outside
Insight
Make the future inhabitable
One scenario, not a forecast
Solution
A speakeasy installation
Six artifacts, one world
Outcome
Hopes and fears, collected
Fed back to New Sun Rising
Challenge

How do you make people feel what a nonprofit is up against?

How might we communicate the need to empower small community organizations to collaborate and thrive, despite significant pressure from government and institutional opposition?

Research

Signals and drivers

We interviewed nonprofit leaders about what they hope for and what they fear in Pittsburgh’s future. The signals we found were local initiatives, elderly engagement, technological shifts, and tightening funding. Those became the reflective questions we asked participants afterward.

Building the future world

We committed to one scenario: nonprofits forced into clandestine, speakeasy-like spaces where people work around the rules. The world has its own details, like the tech acronym drifting from MAMAA to DADAA and climate migration reshaping the city, which is what made the room feel inhabited rather than staged.

Craft

Six artifacts, one world

ID keychains

Participants got an ID modeled on a government-issued EBT card, carrying six sectors on one piece of plastic: health, education, technology, arts, culture, environment, and social work. You chose the identifications you cared about.

Police tracker

A security alert that warned of hostile entities nearby, doubling as a futuristic smart home panel. It read climate temperatures and raised an alarm when police proximity threatened the venue.

Menu of services

Digital and physical menus carried the speakeasy storytelling. Tablets ran a Figma prototype that guests drove with their ID keychains, and the services were personalized: scan your ID and the menu asked how you would bring vibrancy back to your community.

Posters

Outdoor posters warned visitors in somber tones and several languages to disable location tracking. Indoor posters sold the optimistic version of the future: 3D food printing, robotic child care, lab-grown superfoods. Hung eclectically, so the walls looked lived in.

Photobooth

A reimagined job search service. Underground, you could still get a corporate headshot.

Branding

The identity had to travel from repression to hope. A grayscale, minimalist logo with playful edges, bold blocky type, and a palette of deep aqua, lime green, and neon pink for the speakeasy’s own diversity.

The space

A gym, turned speakeasy

String lights and a large sign set the tone. Spray-painted boards split the room into People’s Vertex and the reflection room. Electro swing played underneath. Role play did the rest: we took on bartender, bouncer, and photo booth operator so participants had someone to be in the world with.

Reflections

What participants told us

Two stations caught people on the way out. The first used pink and blue stars to read the room: most found the future dark, with the real work happening at local scale, around institutions rather than through them. The second used double-sided cards for hopes, fears, and expectations, which fed New Sun Rising’s own research.

Takeaway

Constraints stretched our thinking further than open briefs did. Narrowing to one specific future produced stranger, better ideas and much better conversations. We were never trying to predict what happens. We were trying to ask whether it should.

4RUN2

P3R runs Pittsburgh’s premier races, including the DICK’S Sporting Goods Pittsburgh Marathon. This project built the branding and sales strategy for 4RUN2, their in-house apparel line, with the Health & Fitness Expo during race weekend as the main point of sale.

Problem
Runners meet the brand too late
At the expo, after registration
Insight
Start at registration
Where the traffic already is
Solution
Starter kits, a site, a booth
Plus the buggy cart on course
Outcome
A brand that starts earlier
Sales strategy for P3R
Challenge

Runners meet the brand too late to buy it

How might we grow 4RUN2’s online and in-person sales through marketing that starts earlier, and through the point-of-sale moments P3R already owns at its events?

Today a runner registers, picks up a bib, and races. Almost none of that touches the P3R brand or its products, and beyond the occasional email and a single website mention there is nothing else carrying it.

Strategy

Redesigning the experience

Most runners discover the brand at the expo, which is far too late to turn into a purchase. The redesign introduces it earlier through starter kits, social, and a booth you cannot walk past.

Registration and starter kit

A package option offered at registration, with apparel choices, customizable colors and sizes, and room to add more to the kit.

Reach

Partnerships were judged on three things: willingness to actually engage with 4RUN2, real passion for the products, and social following. Coverage runs March through May and June against targets for engagement, visibility, and sales, including a local tour through favorite pizzerias, the Carnegie International, and Steelers games.

Attend the expo, take something home

Runners and supporters leave with something: keychains, tote bags, or digital assets like brochures and branded wallpapers. Small objects, but they are what carries brand recall past race weekend.

Website

One link, a lot of traffic

The Pittsburgh Marathon registration page links to 4RUN2 exactly once. Given the traffic that page already carries, the redesign uses it to position 4RUN2 clearly as P3R’s premium in-house brand and send people straight to shop.

To justify the price, the site had to look like it deserved it: a luxury, pride-driven aesthetic that signals quality through visuals, typography, and storytelling.

Luxury visual system
A design language that reinforces premium positioning and brand pride.
Shopify and SEO
Plugins and search improvements to raise discoverability, traffic, and sales.
Stronger product pages
Size charts, more imagery, fuller descriptions, reviews, and color options to cut friction and returns.
Customer insights
Session recordings and surveys to show real behavior and keep informing the work.
Moonshot

The buggy cart

An advertisement nobody expects, because it is actually useful. Supporters stock supplies and snacks at checkpoints, marathon tracking helps them arrive on time, and they follow their runner along the course instead of waiting at one spot or the finish. Two or three buggies carrying pace teams keep the middle of the race from going quiet.

Brand Voice

AI drafts felt generic and “too much like AI.” I designed a system that learns a brand's voice from its own campaigns, then lets people edit it. Subject-line adoption rose 8.3% with no interface change.

Problem

Two jobs that contradict each other.

Drafts came out generic, so marketers edited by hand. The brief then asked for two opposite things.

Job oneMake it invisible

“Drafts should sound like my brand, without me editing them.”

vs.
Job twoGive me control

“Let me define and review my brand’s tone.”

In a four-person study, no one found the entry point, and no one recognized the descriptors as their own voice.

Challenge

Six things in the way.

Eight findings, scored by severity.

Customers

Mostly a form nobody fills in

A whiteboard on what can be inferred

Four of four never touched the brand voice setting on their own. The modal popover was the suspect.

4

Severity as scored in the report, January 2025, run with our researcher on two prototypes I selected. The top finding is the one I could not design my way out of: a system built to be invisible had succeeded.

Design thinking

The answer lives in a data model, not a screen.

Key insight

Control isn't a mode you switch into. It's a silent escalation. The instant someone edits a descriptor, their words outrank the machine's inference for that field permanently. No settings, no toggle, nothing to discover.

Decisions

A deadlock I was handed.

Option A
Infer from recent campaigns
Drift risk. The voice moves without anyone asking.
Option B
Freeze at creation
Staleness risk. The voice stops when the brand does not.
Shipped
Most recent, plus Freeze
Default moves, with an escape hatch for people who set it once.

An engineer called it a UX question and tagged me. I brought a customer’s own words: they would expect to set this up once.

Craft

Teach the model with examples. Teach the person with labels.

Name (for the person only)
Opinionated
Voice
Description (this is what the model reads)
We assert an informed point of view, grounding that POV in proof and truth. We never go so far as to feel bossy, accusatory or know-it-all.
Like this
“Your life is your business, not ours.”
Not like this
“Do the work to protect yourself online.” (sounds bossy)

The label is for the human, the body is for the machine. The name does nothing to the model; it exists so a person can trust the row. Examples are scoped to long-form only, since the subject-line path is high-volume and latency-sensitive.

The cold-start state, an account that cannot have a voice yet.
Brand voice

Upload a style guideDescribe your voice

A voice can't be inferred below a threshold of sent campaigns, so every new account hits a wall. I retrofitted this after handoff. It routes to the two manual paths rather than apologising.

Results

Same interface. 8% more acceptance.

A pure backend experiment. 50/50 by campaign, ~637,000 messages, no visual difference between the arms.

Why the small number is the interesting one

No new affordance, nothing to discover. Suggestions simply sounded like the merchant, and marketers accepted them 8% more often. The flat open and click rates are the honest counterpart: on-brand copy changed marketer behaviour, not recipient behaviour.

It also stopped being a feature. Built for one surface, it became a service for five, and ownership moved to the orchestration layer where teams inherit voice rather than build it.

Lessons learned

The destructive override shipped. Uploading a style guide discards descriptors the user wrote. I'd now argue for merge-or-confirm.

The descriptor UI may do less work than it looks. An engineer thought the raw examples give the model far better context, which makes the editable list mainly a trust surface. I think he's right, and it isn't what I thought I was building.

The headline target was never measured. The requirements set 60% to 70% content acceptance, and no document reports whether it moved.

Removing is safe. Adding is not.

The next milestone inverts the feature: instead of removing recipients, it adds them. Removing the riskiest one percent is safe on any audience. Data XRay

Athenic lets business teams analyze data in plain English, with no SQL and no data team. The AI worked. People signed up, got confused, and left before they ever saw it work. I led the end-to-end design to make the promise feel real.

Background

Powerful AI. Nobody could use it.

Users signed up, got confused, and left before experiencing any value. Not because the AI failed, but because the experience around it got in the way.

Where it broke down. The customer workflow, mapped from interviews with myKaarma, PMC Studio, BMW and Additel.

Unclear question askingPeople did not know how to phrase a question the AI could answer.
No continuityEvery session started over. No history, no follow-ups.
Fragmented flowsDashboard and search were separate products.
Hidden product value runs through all of it: you could not see enough of the product to know what was possible.
Design thinking

Fewer features. More clarity.

Rather than adding analytical power, I worked on instructional design, discoverability and conversational continuity. I ran a whiteboard session with engineering to map what the system could actually do, and to decide what to build first.

Mapping capabilities with engineering.
Defining the criteria each direction had to satisfy.

Over twelve rounds of user testing I worked through four product directions before converging.

The iteration studies behind them.
Key insight

People did not need more analytical power. They needed clarity, guidance and continuity. The constraint was never the AI. It was the experience around it.

Final design

Ask. See. Remember.

Chat, charts and saved insights in one workbook. Ask a question, see the answer visualized, and build on it without losing your place.

One workbook. Questions and charts in the same place, with no switching between search and dashboard.
Transparent wait time. The part I am most pleased with.

The backend takes a while, so instead of a spinner the interface narrates the work: it pulls the tables first, then draws the graphs, and shows where the data came from. The wait became the moment that built trust rather than the moment people left.

Question history. Continuity within a session and across past ones.

Insights build on each other instead of starting over every time, which is what turned a demo into something people came back to.

Impact

Easier to start, so more people stayed.

Reflection

Powerful AI is only worth something when people understand and trust it. Reframing analytics as a conversation let non-technical teams work with their own data, without an analyst in the room.

SiteMatch

Affordable housing developers spend months evaluating sites by hand, without data. SiteMatch surfaces the best ones by estimated funding score, so developers can find a site and secure the money faster.

Problem
Site selection takes months by hand
Developers evaluate without the data
Insight
Funding score is the real question
Not the site, but whether it wins the money
Solution
Rank sites by estimated score
With the inputs behind the number
Result
Under 12 months
Pre-development cut from years
Background

A process built on guesswork and regulation.

Affordable housing developers win tax credits through the Qualified Allocation Plan, a government score where higher means a better chance. Criteria differ across fifty states, policies keep shifting, and data is scarce, so scoring accurately at speed is nearly impossible.

Scarce funding
Construction costs rise while profits stay capped by rent limits. Every point of score matters.
High demand, low return
The housing is urgently needed, and the process is longer and more complex than market-rate work.
Government dependency
Region-specific policy shifts constantly, and developers have to stay current across several states at once.
No reliable tool
Site evaluation was manual, from scattered sources and outdated information.
Research

Ten expert interviews. One clear opportunity.

We interviewed developers, architects and consultants working in affordable housing on the East Coast to find where the real friction was. The answer was site selection. There was no reliable way to identify and evaluate sites against the funding criteria, so people did it by hand.

What the score is made of. The five criteria developers optimize against.
Where we could actually intervene. Value opportunity analysis across the process.
The seven values developers prioritize.
Design thinking

Three concepts. One winner.

After a build-a-thon with Crazy 8 ideation and physical wireframing, we converged on three concepts, each attacking the problem from a different angle.

Chosen
SiteMatch. Surfaces the best sites by estimated score. It solves the site selection problem directly.
Not chosen
SiteGPT. Conversational guidance for developers. Broader, and much harder to scope to an MVP.
Not chosen
SiteScope. A browser extension to save and analyze sites. Too passive; it never surfaces an opportunity on its own.
How we decided
Co-design workshops with developers, architects and multi-housing experts, then affinity mapping.
Co-design and affinity mapping.
Final design

Find the right site. Score it. Win the funding.

One place for reliable, integrated, current site information, organized around the decision developers actually make: which site gives them the best shot at funding.

Lessons learned

Experience level shapes everything. New developers want simplicity; experienced ones want their existing workflow respected, and those are different products.

Research

The blocker is explainability, not accuracy.

Sixteen power users, five sessions. Trust splits along data-driven versus generative. All sixteen accepted send time, predictive metrics and audience optimization. All sixteen refused to let AI write customer-facing copy unreviewed.

I have it running on two different flows. On one, the personalization is crushing it. On the other, it’s sucking wind, and I’ve got literally no why.

Agency strategist · Agency

Two things changed priorities. Defaults decide adoption, because the controls sit below the scroll line. And the dashboard is glance-once, so the real ask was an alert when something underdelivers.
